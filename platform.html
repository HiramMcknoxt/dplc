The Unified Campaign Platform for Lonoke County

A Blueprint for Resource Stewardship, Government Accountability, and Local Self-Determination — the platform adopted by the Democratic Party of Lonoke County for the 2026 campaign. Full text of each pillar, as adopted.

Pillar 1: Reclaiming Local Control & Solving the City-Limits Pre-emption Crisis

The problem. Through state-level legislation — specifically Act 851 (the Arkansas Data Centers Act) and Act 314 — the state government in Little Rock has overridden local land-use decisions. Act 851 and Act 548 stripped cities and counties of power to place specialized restrictions, noise bans, or environmental limits on high-intensity data centers and crypto mines. Act 314 abolished cities' extraterritorial jurisdiction (ETJ): for decades, towns like Cabot could use a 1-to-3-mile buffer zone outside city limits to manage smart growth; now cities are handcuffed to their borders, and because unincorporated county land is often un-zoned, a developer can build a loud, resource-heavy data center or a poorly drained 200-lot subdivision one foot outside city limits, leaving neighboring residents helpless.

The strategy. Turn the Quorum Court into a defensive shield for our cities and rural areas alike. Since Act 314 hands exclusive planning authority over unincorporated land to the county, deploy county-wide zoning, buffer frameworks, and resource laws so developers can't exploit the border lines — without threatening county residents' freedom from zoning laws.

  • The Unincorporated Border Shield Ordinance: county-wide planning zones extending three miles outward from the borders of all incorporated municipalities (Cabot, Lonoke, Carlisle, Austin, Ward). Major commercial development or high-density housing in these former ETJ zones must pass a county-level planning review.
  • Repealing Corporate Tax Handouts: roll back Act 548, which lowered the bar for massive data-center sales-tax exemptions from $500 million to $100 million. Tax breaks only for businesses that guarantee long-term local jobs and sign binding resource-protection agreements.

Pillar 2: Digital Resource Stewardship & Ratepayer Protection

The problem. Hyperscale computing facilities, AI hubs, and digital-asset mining operations require staggering amounts of electricity and water. State utility and economic packages favor these multi-billion-dollar entities, so citizens face a double threat: water tables drawn down for computer cooling, and multi-state utilities passing the cost of massive new substations onto residential ratepayers' monthly bills.

The strategy. An unyielding "Pay to Play" rule: a digital conglomerate operating here must prove it is entirely self-funded, ecologically neutral, and completely detached from the financial sheets of local families and farmers.

  • Local Utility Cost-Delinking: county ordinances prohibiting utilities from shifting the capital costs of dedicated data-center substations, transmission lines, or grid reinforcements onto residential or small-business rate classes.
  • Mandatory Hydrological Resource Disclosures: developers using liquid or evaporative cooling fund independent, third-party hydrological studies proving daily water extraction will not diminish the water table or disrupt agricultural well pressure.
  • Strict Industrial Noise Thresholds: data infrastructure confined to Heavy Industrial (I-2) zones, with continuous acoustic limits not to exceed 55 decibels (dBA) at the property line.

Pillar 3: Infrastructure, Drainage, & Responsible Growth

The problem. Major projects like the state's $53.9 million Single-Point Urban Interchange (SPUI) upgrade at I-57 and Highway 5 in Cabot are driving unprecedented residential development requests. Recklessly approved high-density subdivisions — such as the contested 212-lot development on Highway 5 — cause severe traffic congestion and alter natural watersheds, forcing hazardous stormwater runoff into neighboring yards and flooding existing properties.

The strategy. Growth must be responsible, planned, and paced — shifting the financial burden of infrastructural strain from long-term residents onto the developers causing the impact.

  • Mandatory Comprehensive Watershed Impact Fees: developers proposing large subdivisions (25+ lots) fund independent hydrological studies; if a project increases downstream runoff or alters drainage, the developer fully funds the necessary drainage infrastructure upgrades before a plat is approved.
  • Traffic Gridlock Mitigation Standards: coordinate county planning with the Arkansas Department of Transportation (ArDOT) to pause high-density rezonings if local feeder roads are operating past safe capacity.

Pillar 4: Evidence Integrity & Law Enforcement Accountability

The problem. Public trust in law enforcement and the judicial system has been shaken by high-profile administrative failures. The court dismissal of a second-degree murder case against a local candidate due to "egregious" law-enforcement misconduct — specifically the failure to properly secure and log vital dash-cam evidence, which subsequently went missing — proves systemic negligence has real impacts on justice, constitutional due process, and public safety.

The strategy. Demand absolute accountability and modern technological standards within county law enforcement. Justice must never again be derailed by administrative incompetence.

  • Strict Evidence Tracking & Chain-of-Custody Audits: modern digital tracking protocols — all body-cam, dash-cam, and physical evidence logged immediately into an immutable, audited digital database rather than kept in unsecure, personal environments.
  • Maximizing the Justice Center Infrastructure: ensure the new $8 million Lonoke County Justice Center is fully leveraged to streamline case backlogs, clear structural inefficiencies, and reduce the fiscal burden of long-term county jail stays.

Pillar 5: Transparent Government & Historic Preservation

The problem. From controversial local council reversals involving public land use (such as placing a school bus complex on historic Carver land) to the abrupt resignation of the Lonoke County Clerk over internal office turmoil and election certification friction, voters are being left in the dark. Government operations must be open, efficient, and free from backroom deals.

The strategy. Restore faith in county governance by opening the books, expanding public notice mandates, and protecting the historic and cultural integrity of our communities.

  • Open Government & Accessible Records: upgrade the county's digital infrastructure so Quorum Court agendas, line-item budgets, and municipal expenditures are completely searchable, machine-readable, and posted online at least one week prior to any public vote.
  • The Historic Neighborhood Protection Act: ordinances requiring an extended 60-day public notice window and a 2/3 supermajority vote by local councils or the Quorum Court before any publicly owned land designated as historically or culturally significant can be rezoned, repurposed, or sold.

Pillar 6: Family Support & Workforce Development

The problem. State mandates under the LEARNS Act place immense pressure on local families by enforcing strict 3rd-grade retention rules based on standardized testing. At the same time, too many young, local talents are forced to commute out of Lonoke County into Pulaski County to find stable, high-paying career paths.

The strategy. Mobilize county resources as a supportive safety net for our families while investing heavily in local technical skills to keep our workforce here at home.

  • The Community Literacy Lifeline: partner with local public libraries (Cabot, Lonoke, Carlisle) to host free, county-subsidized after-school and summer literacy intervention programs tailored directly to state reading benchmarks.
  • Expanding Career and Technical Education (CTE): deeper economic partnerships with local cornerstones like the Lonoke Business Academy to scale up training paths in advanced agriculture, diesel mechanics, industrial technology, and rural healthcare.
